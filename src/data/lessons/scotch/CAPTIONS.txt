Welcome to the Scotch Gambit with Professor Potato Pie, a civilised opening with a slightly mischievous undercurrent.
We shall keep two things in view throughout the course: what the moves threaten, and what the position actually dissolves.
The latter is a useful habit in chess and in choosing an umbrella for a Manchester afternoon.
Start with white pawn to e4, black pawn to e5, white knight to f3, black knight to c6, and white pawn to d4.
Black replies pawn from e5 takes d4 — here comes the fork in the road.
In the regular Scotch Game, white plays knight takes d4, recovers the pawn immediately, and develops in a perfectly respectable manner.
The Scotch Gambit makes a different choice.
White plays bishop to c4 and leaves the d4 pawn alone for a moment.
White buys activity rather than insisting on immediate bookkeeping.
The bishop looks at f7; development can be brisk and Black's king may be asked awkward questions before it castles.
The Scotch Game received serious top-level attention after Kasparov used it in the 1990 World Championship match.
The Gambit is the less common, more practical cousin — not a factory for forced wins.
After bishop to c4, Black has no obligation to panic.
Bishop to c5 develops actively and meets White's bishop; knight to f6 attacks e4 and leads us to part 2.
Black can also choose bishop to b4 check, pawn to d6, or bishop to e7.
A gambit is an invitation to calculate, not a eviction notice.
Back to the position after White's bishop reaches c4.
For an illustrative branch, Black plays bishop to c5 and White tries pawn to c3, offering another pawn while preparing to challenge the centre.
If Black accepts with pawn from d4 takes c3, White has a tidy tactical pattern.
Bishop takes f7 check draws the king forward.
Black answers king takes f7, then White plays queen to d5 check.
The queen checks the exposed king and attacks Black's bishop on c5.
After king to f8, White continues queen takes c5 check, recovering the bishop.
It is a handsome fork, the sort one may place on the mantelpiece — providing one labels it correctly.
The label is this: the sequence does not refute Black's bishop to c5.
At the final display position, Black is objectively fine and White remains a pawn down.
White has gained checks and displaced the king, but Black has material, playable development, and time to consolidate.
Treat c3 as a tactical idea and a reminder to inspect f7 — not as a promised advantage.
Our foundation is therefore simple.
The regular Scotch says recapture now.
The Scotch Gambit says develop first and make the position uncomfortable.
